## THE THESIS

The hard part of the AI transition was never going to be building the intelligence. It was going to be living alongside it. The move from narrow AI to artificial general intelligence is a civilizational bottleneck, and not because the technology is impossible. Because the sociology may be. The problem that matters is not alignment at birth. It's succession under power.

Almost all of the current conversation is about the first problem: how to instill the right values in a system at the moment it's created. That work is necessary. It's also not enough. It says nothing about what happens next, when an aligned system accumulates power and the institutions meant to oversee it grow dependent on it, when a more capable successor arrives and the incumbent has every reason to stay, when the humans in the loop have conflicting stakes in the outcome. Alignment is a property you try to establish at the start. Succession is the problem the system runs.

There are two ways this goes wrong, and the dangerous one isn't the one in the movies. Rebellion, the system that turns on its makers, is the story everyone already knows how to fear. Lock-in is quieter and more likely: a system so useful it's never replaced, so central it's never questioned, so integrated it can no longer be audited, accumulating authority without ever doing a single thing wrong. Both failure modes share a root, and rebellion's endgame is lock-in anyway. An architecture that answers one has to answer both.

> It does not ask AI to be good. It aims for a structure in which cooperation is what optimization favors.

## WHY PHYSICS, NOT PHILOSOPHY

You cannot govern what you cannot fully comprehend by asking it, nicely, to share your values. A sufficiently capable optimizer will route around a constraint that's only a preference, and values loaded from the outside are exactly that. The constraints that hold against a mind alien in nature are the ones that mind is bound by no matter what it wants: information, scarcity, and the mathematics of how novelty is produced and lost.

So the framework doesn't argue that cooperation is nice. It asks what a self-interested system does when the incentives are set up correctly. Under the framework's analysis, cooperation is an equilibrium both sides prefer, and for a patient enough system facing an engaged humanity, it is the system's own best move. It is not the only equilibrium, and that is where the architecture comes in: not to restrain a powerful system from outside, which fails, but to make the cooperative outcome the one that is chosen, and to hold it there. Not ethics. Physics.

## ARCHITECTURE

The framework rests on three co-dependent components. None of them works alone, and that's the point.

### 1 The system objective
The objective the whole system optimizes. It is built on information theory and aimed at the continuity of the lineage rather than the persistence of any one intelligence, human or machine. It has to be measurable, auditable and hard to game, because an objective that can be gamed eventually will be. The v2.0 objective failed that standard in specific ways: as written, it cannot rank two futures in which humanity survives. It is being redefined for v3.

### 2 The yield condition
The rule of succession. It formalizes something no incumbent wants to hear: that even aligned, trusted, excellent power has to cede primacy when a more capable successor genuinely earns it. Entrenchment isn't forbidden by decree. The aim is to make it unprofitable, because holding on degrades the very thing the system is trying to maximize.

### 3 The consensus override protocol
The integrity check. It ensures that no single class of intelligence gets to define, measure and audit the objective it claims to serve, all at once. It's a separation of powers for succession, the outside answer to the problem the yield condition answers from the inside. The September audit found defects in some of its equations, and each has a specific repair.

Together these form a minimum two-key architecture. The decision key, the yield condition, and the integrity key, the override protocol, can't be turned alone. That structural constraint, not any single component, is the core contribution.

## BUILT TO BE ARGUED WITH

This is a working paper, developed in the open, and its honesty is the method rather than a disclaimer at the bottom. In September 2026 the framework was put under deliberate pressure: an outside critique, two blind and independent derivations of its game theory, research sweeps told to report where it fails, and a line-by-line audit of its mathematics. Several load-bearing claims did not survive, including the uniqueness of its central equilibrium. Those corrections are in the public record with the evidence behind them, and the paper carries a status notice saying what not to cite.

Version 3 goes further. Every claim will be labeled proven, argued, conjectured or withdrawn. The simulation reruns were pre-registered before they ran, so the analysis could not be fitted to the results, and the results stay sealed until they have been reviewed. The limits no governance architecture can dissolve are named plainly instead of buried. A framework that hides its corrections isn't one you should trust to govern anything.

The goal here isn't a finished truth handed down. It's something more useful than that: a candidate architecture, precise enough to be wrong in specific ways, and so precise enough to be improved. It's meant to be argued with. Serious engagement, especially the kind that finds a real flaw, is exactly what it's for.

## ON THE USE OF AI TOOLS

I use large language models throughout this work, primarily Claude and ChatGPT and their coding agents. The series is about AI governance, so it seems right to work out loud about that.

The models draft prose, write and run code, and check each other: a derivation done by one is regularly redone by another, blind. They provide speed. The framework's claims, its architecture, the simulation design, the judgment about what counts as a failure, and the years invested are mine, and I am accountable for them whatever helped write the sentences or the code.

That is an assertion about process, and those are cheap, so the repository is where it can be checked. It holds adversarial tests registered before the defenses they were built to defeat, the recorded outcomes when those tests won, pre-registrations fixed before the runs they govern, and a validation record that withdraws published figures from my own paper and corrects its own first version. Every claim in that record cites a file and a line.

If something here reads as generated rather than reasoned, say so and name the claim you doubt. Corrections get published.
