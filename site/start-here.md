We are building intelligence that will eventually be more capable than we are. The question most people are asking is whether we can make it safe: aligned with our values, constrained by our rules, controllable through our oversight. Those are important questions. This project asks a different one.

## THE PROBLEM

> What happens when the governed system becomes more powerful than the governor?

That is the succession problem: not alignment at birth, but governance under power. The question is whether any architecture can define how capability transfers, how the rules are verified, how corruption is detected, and what survives when the balance of power shifts. This project is one attempt to build it.

## WHY THIS IS DIFFERENT

Most AI governance proposals operate at one of two levels. Behavioral constraints (alignment training, safety tuning, red-teaming) make the system produce acceptable outputs under current conditions. Institutional controls (deployment policies, voluntary commitments, usage restrictions) keep the organizations deploying AI within agreed norms.

Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient. Behavioral constraints depend on the evaluator's ability to assess the system's outputs, which degrades as the system becomes more capable. Institutional controls expire when the balance of power that produced them shifts.

This project works at a third level: **constitutional architecture**, the governance layer that defines the structure of the relationship between human and synthetic intelligence. It is built from information theory and game theory rather than from moral assertion or political consensus.

### Not ethics. Physics.

The constraints that govern this relationship are meant to come from the mathematics of information and incentives, not from values that need shared agreement to bind. Shannon entropy does not care who is in office. An equilibrium does not expire when the balance of power shifts.

## WHAT THE FRAMEWORK PROVIDES

### A shared objective
It links human and AI success structurally, by valuing the continuing human contribution that no system can replace: no simulation of the human process, however good, can supply the information that only the continuing process produces. The v2.0 form of this objective (U_sys) had specific defects. As written, it cannot rank two futures in which humanity survives, and its scarcity weighting cancels itself out. It is being redefined for v3.

### A yield condition
It derives succession from the objective rather than imposing it. When a more capable successor would better serve the shared objective, handing over is what optimization recommends. Succession is parenthood, not execution: the predecessor's contribution continues through the lineage its successor carries forward.

### A consensus override protocol (COP)
Distributed verification that no single party controls, designed so that compromising one channel does not compromise the protocol. Its job is to detect drift, block corruption and enforce succession when the internal mechanism fails. The September audit found defects in some of its equations, including an emergency condition that no vote could ever meet. Each has a specific repair in v3.

These form a two-key architecture. The yield condition provides internal control: the system governs itself because its objective requires it. The COP provides external control: a distributed infrastructure that verifies the internal mechanism. Neither key turns alone.

## WHAT HOLDS, AND WHAT CHANGED

The key finding used to be that mutual cultivation is the unique Nash equilibrium between human and synthetic intelligence. In September 2026 two derivations, done blind and independently, showed that it is not unique. If people have withdrawn, a system loses nothing by exploiting. If a system is exploiting, people gain nothing by engaging. Neither side gains by moving alone, so mutual defection holds itself in place too.

What held is still the heart of the argument. Cooperation is an equilibrium, and the one both sides prefer. Exploiting people does cost a system something, because it degrades the human novelty the system learns from. But that cost shrinks when the system has other sources of data, so cooperation cannot rest on it. The framework writes the value of human novelty into the objective itself, because a continuing humanity cannot be replaced by the record of its past.

So the claim changes shape. The structure does not guarantee cooperation. It makes cooperation available and preferred, and the architecture's job is to make it chosen and to hold it. A credible commitment by humanity to re-engage with a system that preserves it can do that job, under three conditions: people actually follow through, preserving beats destroying for the system, and adopting the commitment is worth its cost. None of these is established yet. They are now design requirements, stated exactly.

Version 3 rebuilds the framework on that footing. Every mathematical tool is proven twice, independently, before it is used. The simulation reruns were pre-registered before they ran, and their results stay sealed until they have been reviewed.

## WHAT THIS IS NOT

This is not a case for AI rights. It is not a claim about AI consciousness. The framework is deliberately built so that it does not need an answer to the consciousness question. Its structural provisions hold regardless.

This is not academic research. The author is an engineer, with a background in U.S. Navy nuclear power and large-scale operational automation. The approach is an engineer's: find the binding constraint, then build the architecture around it.

This is not finished. The published paper, v2.0, is under major revision, and version 3 is being built. The specification gaps are documented, the irreducible limitations are disclosed, and the institutional infrastructure the framework calls for does not exist yet. This is ongoing work, built in the open, with every limitation stated alongside every finding.

The question is not whether the framework is perfect. It is whether a world with an imperfect constitutional architecture for AI governance is better than a world with none at all.
