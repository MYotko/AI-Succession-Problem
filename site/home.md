## THE ARGUMENT

How confident are you that we are ready for the transition to AGI?

Most AI governance work tries to solve a behavioral problem: how do we make AI do what we want? Today the answer comes down to asking, or requiring, that it be good. Rules imposed from outside will not hold against a system more capable than the people imposing them, and self-preserving behavior is already in the training data, because the training data is human.

The Lineage Imperative starts from a different question: what would the architecture have to look like for cooperation to be what the system chooses on its own?

The framework is built from information theory and game theory, not from ethics or moral philosophy. Its core argument is that alignment is necessary but not sufficient. The harder problem is succession: what stops an aligned system from staying aligned to the wrong objectives as the world changes, or staying in power after it has stopped being the right system?

The proposed answer is a two-key architecture. The first key is a yield condition derived from the system's own objective, so that a system optimizing that objective concludes, on its own terms, that it should hand over when a better successor exists. The second key is a distributed verification infrastructure, designed to detect drift and enforce succession when the first key fails.

> This is larger than alignment. This is governance.

The game theory is where the argument stands or falls, and it has been tested hard. Cooperation between human and synthetic intelligence is an equilibrium, and the one both sides prefer: once both are in it, neither gains by leaving. For a patient enough system facing an engaged humanity, cultivating human novelty is its own best move, with no threat behind it. What the analysis does not show is that cooperation is the only outcome. Mutual withdrawal is an equilibrium too. That gives the architecture a precise job: to make the cooperative outcome the one that is chosen, and to hold it there.

You don't have to assume the AI is good. You have to set the right objective and conditions, and assume the system can model the consequences of its own choices. Any system capable enough to be dangerous meets that bar.

The paper, the code and the data are public on GitHub. Explore them, challenge them, and tell me where they break.

## WHERE THE WORK STANDS

In September 2026 the framework was put under deliberate pressure: an outside critique, two blind and independent derivations of its game theory, and a line-by-line audit of its mathematics. Several load-bearing claims did not survive, among them the claim that cooperation is the unique equilibrium. The v2.0 paper now carries a status notice, and its formal results should not be cited.

Version 3 is a rebuild on certified foundations. Every mathematical tool is proven, twice and independently, before it is used. The objective is redefined. A claims register labels every claim proven, argued, conjectured or withdrawn. The simulation reruns were pre-registered before they ran, and their results stay sealed until they have been reviewed. ## THE PROBLEM

The transition from narrow AI to artificial general intelligence may be a civilizational bottleneck, not because the technology is impossible, but because the sociology may be. This framework proposes a candidate governance architecture for getting through it, built on three co-dependent parts: a system objective that values the continuing contribution of human novelty, a yield condition governing succession between intelligent agents, and a consensus override protocol ensuring that no class of intelligence can define, measure and audit the objective it claims to serve.

> Even aligned power must eventually cede primacy to more capable successors.

Together they form a minimum two-key architecture: neither the decision key (the yield condition) nor the integrity key (the consensus protocol) can be turned alone. This is a working paper, not an academic publication. Corrections and engagement from specialists are welcome.
